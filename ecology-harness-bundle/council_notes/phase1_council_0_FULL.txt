## Framework: COM-B

- **Canonical sources:** Michie S, van Stralen MM, West R. "The behaviour change wheel: A new method for characterising and designing behaviour change interventions." *Implementation Science* 6:42, 2011 — **VERIFIED** (full text retrieved this session). Note: the task's separate entry "Michie, van Stralen & West 2011" is the **same paper** (COM-B is introduced there as the hub of the BCW); not a second source. The practical guide (Michie, Atkins & West, *The Behaviour Change Wheel: A Guide to Designing Interventions*, Silverback, 2014) — **UNVERIFIED** (not fetched). Acronym coined by Marie Johnston (paper acknowledgements) — **VERIFIED**.

- **Core constructs:** Behaviour arises from an interacting "behaviour system" of three conditions, each with two sub-components (all definitions verbatim from 2011 paper): **Capability** — the individual's psychological (knowledge, comprehension, cognitive skill) or physical (bodily skill/strength) capacity to engage in the activity; **Opportunity** — all factors outside the individual that make behaviour possible or prompt it, physical (environment, resources, time) or social (cultural milieu, norms, language); **Motivation** — "all those brain processes that energize and direct behaviour," reflective (evaluations, plans, intentions) or automatic (habits, emotions, impulses from associative learning and innate dispositions); motivation definition drawn from Mook (1995), dual-process grounding from Strack & Deutsch (2004) — **VERIFIED**.

- **Claimed mechanism:** For a given behaviour in a given context, changing one or more of the six components is the route to behaviour change; the components causally interact (opportunity can influence motivation; capability can influence motivation) and enacted behaviour feeds back to alter capability, motivation, and opportunity. The 2011 paper derives the triad from two convergent traditions: Fishbein et al.'s necessary/sufficient prerequisites for volitional behaviour (skills + strong intention + no environmental constraint) and US criminal law's means/opportunity/motive, extended to non-volitional processes (habit, impulse) — **VERIFIED**.

- **Signature methods/instruments:** COM-B as **diagnosis**: specify the behavioural target, then profile which components need to change (the paper's guiding question: "What conditions internal to individuals and in their social and physical environment need to be in place for a specified behavioural target to be achieved?"). It functions as the hub of the BCW: Table 2 links each COM-B component to candidate intervention functions (e.g., C-Ps ← education/persuasion; M-Au ← incentivisation/coercion/environmental restructuring/modelling; O-Ph ← training/restriction/environmental restructuring/enablement) — **VERIFIED**. Intervention functions in turn link to seven policy categories (Table 3) — **VERIFIED**. No standardized COM-B measurement instrument exists in the 2011 paper; later studies proxy items via the Theoretical Domains Framework.

- **Evidence landmarks:** (1) 2011 development study: systematic review found 19 frameworks, **none** covering the full range of intervention functions/policies; only a minority coherent or linked to a model of behaviour — **VERIFIED**. (2) 2011 reliability: inter-rater agreement 88% coding England's 2010 Tobacco Control Strategy (24 components), 79% for NICE 2006 obesity guidance (21 components); practitioner agreement vs gold standard 85% (DoH lead) and 75% (researcher) — **VERIFIED**. (3) 2021 Willmott, Pang & Rundle-Thiele (*BMC Public Health*): COM-B explained 31% of variance in young adults' physical activity and 23% in eating; motivation mediated capability→behaviour and opportunity→behaviour, partially validating the hypothesised causal structure — **VERIFIED** (abstract). **Contrary findings:** Ogden (2016, *Health Psychology Review*) argues the COM-B/BCW systematisation "may be neither feasible nor desirable," citing gaps in the protocol-coding→behaviour translational chain — **VERIFIED** (abstract; pre-2020, flagged). Caveat: Willmott's samples were ~80% female young adults; generalization limited — **RECONSTRUCTED** (from abstract demographics).

- **Tensions/open questions:** COM-B is a descriptive model, not a predictive theory — it cannot say *which* component will matter most without further data or theory (the 2011 paper itself contrasts this with intervention mapping's covariation-based logic). Component interactions are asserted qualitatively, not quantified. No canonical instrument, thresholds, or temporal dynamics (habit formation) are specified. The six-way split can collapse distinct mechanisms (e.g., all habits vs. all emotions under M-Au) — **RECONSTRUCTED** synthesis of verified claims.

- **Agent-operability notes:** (**RECONSTRUCTED** — inference for agent use.) COM-B is a fixed six-label taxonomy, ideal as a **typed-node classifier**: an LLM can parse user statements into C-Ph/C-Ps/M-Re/M-Au/O-Ph/O-So ("I don't know how"→C-Ps; "I keep forgetting"→M-Au; "no time/energy"→O-Ph; "my friends would judge me"→O-So). The feedback arrows justify modelling interventions as perturbations with downstream effects. Output slots directly into BCW Table 2 as deterministic graph edges.

- **Home-lab relevance:** (**RECONSTRUCTED**.) Default behaviour-diagnosis front-end for a personal-development agent: each user goal maps to a target behaviour; utterances plus habit-tracker telemetry feed the C/O/M classifier, answering "why am I not doing X" before any tactic is chosen.

- **Source fidelity flags:** 2011 full text and all construct definitions/mechanism/reliability figures VERIFIED by direct fetch. Willmott 2021 abstract VERIFIED; its full discussion not read. Ogden 2016 abstract VERIFIED. 2014 guide, Mook (1995), Strack & Deutsch (2004) contents UNVERIFIED (cited by the 2011 paper, not fetched). Agent-operability and home-lab sections RECONSTRUCTED.

## Framework: Behavior Change Wheel (BCW)

- **Canonical sources:** Michie, van Stralen & West, *Implementation Science* 6:42, 2011 — **VERIFIED** (full text retrieved). The 2014 Silverback guide (Michie, Atkins, West) is the operational "how-to" companion — **UNVERIFIED** (not fetched). The BCW was constructed *in the same paper* as COM-B: 19 existing frameworks → 9 intervention functions + 7 policy categories → assembled as a three-layer wheel.

- **Core constructs:** Three concentric layers: **hub** = the COM-B behaviour system; **middle** = nine intervention functions, defined as non-overlapping classes of means: Education (increasing knowledge/understanding), Persuasion (communication to induce feelings or stimulate action), Incentivisation (creating expectation of reward), Coercion (creating expectation of punishment/cost), Training (imparting skills), Restriction (rules reducing opportunity), Environmental restructuring (changing physical/social context), Modelling (providing an example to imitate), Enablement (increasing means/reducing barriers beyond the above) — all definitions **VERIFIED** from Table 1. **Outer** = seven policy categories enabling interventions: Communication/marketing, Guidelines, Fiscal measures, Regulation, Legislation, Environmental/social planning, Service provision — **VERIFIED**. An intervention may serve several functions simultaneously; a given function can be enabled by several policies.

- **Claimed mechanism:** Behaviour change requires changing one or more COM-B components; intervention functions are the classes of means by which components are changed (Table 2 matrix); policy categories are how authorities enable/support those functions (Table 3 matrix). The wheel thus encodes a causal chain policies → interventions → behaviour system, grounded in a single unifying theory of "motivation in context" (West, *Theory of Addiction*, 2006) that spans reflective and automatic processes — **VERIFIED**. The 2011 paper explicitly frames BCTs as the fine-grained content *inside* functions ("any one intervention function is likely to comprise many individual BCTs, and the same BCT may serve different intervention functions") while admitting "reliable taxonomies for BCTs within these intervention functions have yet to be developed" — **VERIFIED**.

- **Signature methods/instruments:** Systematic characterisation of existing interventions by function/policy coding; the paper's own reliability-testing procedure (independent coding, inter-rater agreement, gold-standard adjudication) — **VERIFIED**. Design logic: target behaviour → COM-B diagnosis → select intervention function(s) → select policy category(ies) → later, specific BCTs. Explicit contrast with intervention mapping (Bartholomew et al.): BCW asks "what conditions need to be in place" (causal analysis); IM asks "what factors underlie variation in the behavioural parameter" (epidemiological covariation) — **VERIFIED**.

- **Evidence landmarks:** (1) 2011: none of the 19 reviewed frameworks met all three usefulness criteria (comprehensiveness, coherence, linkage to a behaviour model); the constructed BCW did — **VERIFIED**. (2) 2011 reliability: 88% inter-rater on the tobacco strategy, 79% on NICE obesity guidance; 85%/75% practitioner-gold-standard agreement — **VERIFIED**. (3) 2011: BCW used in UK parliamentary briefings and to critique the nudge-only policy stance — **VERIFIED**. (4) Post-2020 application: UNICEF Innocenti/London Met toolkit (2023) operationalises BCW for COVID-19 vaccine uptake and mask-wearing, mapping barriers to functions and implementation strategies — **VERIFIED** (document retrieved). **Contrary/critiques:** Ogden (2016) — over-systematisation argument (VERIFIED abstract). Peters & Kok, "All models are wrong, but some are useful" (2016) — response defending models; **title only VERIFIED** via PubMed, content unverified. Halonen (2024, non-peer-reviewed commentary): BCW "too broad," lacks predictive specificity and comparative empirical validation — flagged as **secondary/UNVERIFIED** (peer-review status).

- **Tensions/open questions:** The 2011 authors themselves state the open question: "how far the BCW can lead to more efficient design of effective interventions" — the framework is descriptive-organizing, not validated as predictive of effectiveness. Function selection depends on judgment; the mapping matrices are theory-derived, not empirically calibrated. Boundary fuzziness between education/persuasion/training in practice. Policy layer assumes authority-level actors — mostly irrelevant at individual scale.

- **Agent-operability notes:** (**RECONSTRUCTED**.) The 9×7 fixed vocabulary is a clean typed-edge schema; Table 2 can be encoded as a static matrix from COM-B diagnosis → recommended functions, giving deterministic, explainable selector behaviour. The "canvass the full range of options" principle counteracts LLM recency/familiarity bias toward education/persuasion. Policy layer can be pruned for personal use (only environmental/social planning and service provision analogues apply to a single user's life).

- **Home-lab relevance:** (**RECONSTRUCTED**.) The design layer: after COM-B diagnosis the agent selects functions (knowledge gap → education; skill gap → training; ambivalence → incentivisation/persuasion; habit failure → environmental restructuring + modelling), yielding multi-component plans instead of single-tactic advice, and records which functions were deployed per goal for later outcome analysis.

- **Source fidelity flags:** All 2011 content (constructs, definitions, matrices, reliability, parliamentary use) VERIFIED from fetched full text. UNICEF 2023 VERIFIED as retrieved report. Peters & Kok title-only; Halonen non-peer-reviewed commentary — both flagged. 2014 guide and West (2006) contents UNVERIFIED. Agent-operability/home-lab sections RECONSTRUCTED.

## Framework: BCT Taxonomy v1

- **Canonical sources:** Michie S, Richardson M, Johnston M, et al. "The Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy (v1) of 93 Hierarchically Clustered Techniques: Building an International Consensus for the Reporting of Behavior Change Interventions." *Annals of Behavioral Medicine* 46(1):81–95, 2013 — **VERIFIED** (abstract via PubMed/Springer; official taxonomy table, the paper's Additional File, retrieved from the Society of Behavioral Medicine). Full methods/discussion text paywalled — partial.

- **Core constructs:** 93 consensually defined behaviour change techniques — the "active content" of interventions — hierarchically clustered into 16 groups: 1 Goals and planning; 2 Feedback and monitoring; 3 Social support; 4 Shaping knowledge; 5 Natural consequences; 6 Comparison of behaviour; 7 Associations; 8 Repetition and substitution; 9 Comparison of outcomes; 10 Reward and threat; 11 Regulation; 12 Antecedents; 13 Identity; 14 Scheduled consequences; 15 Self-belief; 16 Covert learning — **VERIFIED** from the official table. Each BCT has a verb-based label, definition, examples, and disambiguation notes (e.g., 1.4 Action planning "includes Implementation Intentions"; 2.3 Self-monitoring of behaviour; 12.1 Restructuring the physical environment; 15.1 Verbal persuasion about capability). The table notes an additional technique ("Increase positive emotions") deferred to v2 — **VERIFIED**.

- **Claimed mechanism:** The taxonomy is a specification/reporting instrument, not a causal theory. Its premise (from the abstract) is that interventions' content can be characterized "with precision and specificity," with BCTs grouped "according to similarity of active ingredients" — **VERIFIED**. The implicit causal claim — each BCT alters or redirects the causal processes regulating behaviour — is not unpacked in v1; which BCT works through which mechanism is a *separate* evidence question (the later Mechanisms of Action project; e.g., PMC6636886, **title only VERIFIED** via search, content unverified).

- **Signature methods/instruments:** Delphi-type exercise: 14 experts rated labels/definitions of 124 BCTs drawn from six published classification systems; 18 experts then open-sorted BCTs by active-ingredient similarity; six researchers coded 85 intervention descriptions to test reliability — **VERIFIED** (abstract). Result: 93 BCTs in 16 groups; of the 26 BCTs occurring ≥5 times, 23 had adjusted kappas ≥0.60 — **VERIFIED**. The taxonomy table (retrieved) functions as the field's coding manual; a v2 and web resources exist — **UNVERIFIED** (not fetched).

- **Evidence landmarks:** (1) 2013: the consensus taxonomy itself (93/16, kappa figures) — **VERIFIED**. (2) Antecedent evidence cited in the paper's reference list (titles VERIFIED only, content unverified): West et al. 2010 — BCTs used by English Stop Smoking Services associated with short-term quit outcomes; Michie et al. 2009 — meta-regression identifying effective techniques in healthy eating/physical activity. (3) Post-2020 uptake: BCT coding now standard in implementation science (e.g., "Changing behaviour, 'more or less': do implementation and de-implementation interventions include different behaviour change techniques?", *Implementation Science* 2021 — **title only VERIFIED**). **Contrary findings:** Ogden (2016) — coding a protocol cannot capture the translation to actual behaviour; over-standardisation reduces practitioners to "technicians" (**VERIFIED** abstract); the published rebuttal debate (Teixeira 2016; Peters & Kok 2016; Abraham 2016 — **titles VERIFIED** via PubMed comment list, contents unverified).

- **Tensions/open questions:** Reliability is moderate and context-dependent; coding requires trained coders. The taxonomy captures *content*, not dose, quality, fidelity, or delivery skill. The 16-group hierarchy rests on expert judgment, not empirical clustering. Kappa ≥0.60 is a conventional, modest bar. Some techniques (e.g., 16.x covert learning) are awkward for digital/daily-life delivery. BCT–mechanism links remain an open research programme.

- **Agent-operability notes:** (**RECONSTRUCTED**.) The 93 BCTs with stable IDs (e.g., "1.4", "12.1") are directly representable as **atomic skill nodes**; definitions + examples give the LLM deterministic coding heuristics; the disambiguation "Notes" (e.g., "only code goal-setting if sufficient evidence…") are directly usable as rule constraints. BCT groups give typed edges to BCW functions; an agent can emit intervention plans as ordered BCT sequences (goal setting → action planning → self-monitoring → feedback/review) and retro-code delivered plans for measurement.

- **Home-lab relevance:** (**RECONSTRUCTED**.) The agent's tactic library and measurement language: every suggested action carries a BCT code; because the user is one individual, the agent can run an N-of-1 loop — log BCTs actually executed, correlate with outcome data over weeks, and retire ineffective techniques — the empirical layer the field itself lacks at scale.

Full 2013 methods/discussion text NOT retrieved (paywall) — statements beyond abstract/table are RECONSTRUCTED. Antecedent studies and 2016 commentary titles VERIFIED via reference lists, contents UNVERIFIED. v2 and web tools UNVERIFIED.

**Synthesis:** The three form a nested pipeline: COM-B diagnoses *why* the behaviour is not happening (six typed components), the BCW selects the intervention functions and policies that could change the deficient components (two deterministic mapping matrices), and BCTv1 supplies the 93 atomic techniques that instantiate those functions in concrete, codeable actions. For an agent architecture the highest-value transferable elements are (1) the six-component COM-B classifier for barrier diagnosis from user language, (2) the static COM-B→function mapping table as typed graph edges, and (3) the 93-BCT vocabulary as a canonical, ID-stable library of atomic operations with built-in disambiguation rules. The trio is descriptive, not predictive — the authors' own open question is whether the BCW yields *more efficient design of effective* interventions — so a well-behaved agent should treat function/BCT selection as hypothesis generation, log what it actually delivered, and close the loop with the user's outcome data.
