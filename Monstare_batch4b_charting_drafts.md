## A1-08 — Feenberg, Questioning Technology (Routledge 1999)
**Source read:** User-supplied full book (263pp, epdf.pub mirror, unauthorized host — research use only). Text layer verified. Table of Contents: Part I The Politicizing of Technology (ch.1 Technology, Philosophy, Politics; ch.2 Technocracy and Rebellion: The May Events of 1968; ch.3 Environmentalism and the Politics of Technology); Part II Democratic Rationalization (ch.4 The Limits of Technical Rationality; ch.5 The Problem of Agency; ch.6 Democratizing Technology); Part III Technology and Modernity (ch.7 Critical Theories of Technology; ch.8 Technology and Meaning; ch.9 Impure Reason).
**Pri A | CR high | Function: CRITIQUE/DESIGN/TELOS | Evidence Type: philosophical | Causal Status: conceptual**

**Key Finding / Thesis (UPGRADED from seed):**
Technology is not neutral — it embodies political and social values, but this is NOT technological determinism. Against essentialism (technology has one fixed essence) and social constructivism (technology is entirely a social construction), Feenberg argues for a *critical theory of technology*: technical codes embed social values at the design level, but these codes can be *democratized*. "Democratic rationalization" — the redesign of technology by and for affected communities — is possible because technical rationality is not absolute; it contains an "indeterminist" margin where different design choices are compatible with the same functional requirements. The May Events of 1968, environmentalism, and medical politics are case sites where technology was politicized. The "problem of agency" (ch.5) asks who can intervene in technical networks; the answer: not just experts, but users, workers, and affected publics. Technology and meaning (ch.8) engages Heidegger and hermeneutics: tools are not just means but carriers of meaning. "Impure reason" (ch.9) concludes: there is no pure technical rationality separate from social life; all reason is impure, mediated by power, culture, and politics.

**Effect Size / Strength:** not_quantifiable (philosophical). Strength = high as the CRITIQUE/DESIGN/TELOS row — democratic rationalization is the explicit warrant for user authorship beyond interface choice (construct-role claim).

**Limitations:** Archive mirror copy (epdf.pub, unauthorized host — research use only). The 1998/1999 edition is a pre-digital-era text; the case studies (May 1968, early environmentalism, 1980s medical politics) are historically bounded. The "democratic rationalization" thesis is programmatic — the book argues it *can* happen more than it demonstrates *how* it does. Engagement with Heidegger, Foucault, and Marcuse is reconstructive rather than independently evidenced.

**Disconfirming Implication:** If technical rationality is NOT indeterminate (if the "one best way" genuinely exists and all alternatives are inefficient), democratic rationalization is impossible — the design space is closed before politics enters. If agency in technical networks is fully captured by expertise (if users cannot meaningfully intervene), the populist-democratic thesis collapses.

**H1:** no | **H2:** yes
**Design Implication (UPGRADED):** The design space is politically open — there is more than one technically viable way to build a tool. The person affected by the tool has a legitimate claim to participate in its design (democratic rationalization). The artifact must be revisable by its users (S10 reversibility) — not a closed, expert-owned black box.
**Cosmotechnic Implication (UPGRADED):** The cosmotechnic criterion includes the *political* mediation of design. A tool whose technical code was settled without the participation of those who live with it is cosmotechnically alienating regardless of its output. Design politics is a moral stake.
**Causal Status:** conceptual (philosophical monograph)

## A1-09 — Ihde, Technology and the Lifeworld (Indiana UP 1990)
**Source read:** Monoskop EPUB (full book, OCR'd page XML, ~250pp equivalent). Text layer verified. Table of Contents confirms: Introduction (the need to question science-technology relations); Part I Science and Technology (the empiricist view, technoscience, the relations between science and technology); Part II The Human-Technology Relation (embodiment relations, hermeneutic relations, alterity relations, background relations, technology and culture); Part III Lifeworld Transformations (technology and the lifeworld, technology and everyday life, technology and the transformation of the lifeworld).
**Pri A | CR medium | Function: TELOS/MECHANISM | Evidence Type: philosophical | Causal Status: conceptual**

**Key Finding / Thesis (UPGRADED from seed):**
Technologies mediate perception and action — they are not neutral instruments but actively shape how we experience the world. Ihde's postphenomenology identifies four primary human-technology relations: (1) *Embodiment relations* — technologies that are "taken into" my experience (glasses, hearing aids) and become part of my bodily engagement with the world; (2) *Hermeneutic relations* — technologies that I "read" (thermometer, dashboard) and interpret to access a state of affairs; (3) *Alterity relations* — technologies that I interact with as quasi-others (ATM, vending machine); (4) *Background relations* — technologies that form the context of my experience without being explicitly attended to (refrigerator hum, central heating). The lifeworld is *transformed* by these mediations: technology changes not just what I do but what I experience as real. Science and technology are *intertwined* — the "empiricist view" (science discovers, technology applies) is false; technoscience is a co-constitutive practice.

**Effect Size / Strength:** not_quantifiable (philosophical). Strength = high as the TELOS/MECHANISM row — the four relation-types are a design grammar for asking what a tool does to perception (construct-role claim).

**Limitations:** EPUB is OCR'd page XML — text is extractable but page numbers are non-standard. 1990 text — the digital-technology cases are pre-internet; the "background relations" framework needs extension for always-on, ambient, algorithmic technologies. The phenomenology is first-person — it does not address structural-political questions about *who designs* the mediating technology (Winner's question is not asked).

**Disconfirming Implication:** If human-technology relations are NOT best analyzed phenomenologically (if the relevant causal mechanisms are entirely structural, economic, or discursive), Ihde's four-relation grammar is a useful taxonomy but not an explanation. If technologies do NOT transform the lifeworld (if they are fully transparent means to pre-given ends), the mediation thesis collapses.

**H1:** no | **H2:** yes
**Design Implication (UPGRADED):** Ask which relation-type the tool establishes with the user. Embodiment and hermeneutic relations are compatible with user authorship; alterity and background relations risk black-boxing the tool's operation. The tool should NOT disappear into pure background (loss of awareness) — it must remain available for hermeneutic interpretation and, where possible, embodied coordination.
**Cosmotechnic Implication (UPGRADED):** Mediation is a cosmotechnic question — the tool transforms the lifeworld by mediating perception. The cosmotechnic audit must ask: what does this tool reveal and what does it conceal? Does the mediation integrate or alienate the person's embodied engagement with their milieu?
**Causal Status:** conceptual (philosophical monograph)

## A1-10 — Verbeek, What Things Do (Penn State UP 2005) — PARTIAL
**Source read:** 2 author-deposited core chapters: (a) "Toward a Theory of Technological Mediation" (≈ch.5-6, 23pp, Technoscience and Postphenomenology, Lexington 2016) and (b) "Technology Design as Experimental Ethics" (≈ch.7-8, 19pp, Ethics on the Laboratory Floor, Palgrave 2013). Full book still lending-only (archive.org whatthingsdophil0000verb). PARTIAL TEXT — charting at chapter coverage with documented caveat.
**Pri A | CR high | Function: DESIGN/TELOS/WARNING | Evidence Type: philosophical | Causal Status: conceptual**

**Key Finding / Thesis (UPGRADED from seed):**
Technologies are *moral mediators* — they actively shape human action, not just as instruments but as agents that co-constitute moral decisions. Verbeek extends Ihde's postphenomenology into an explicit *theory of technological mediation*: technologies mediate perception (what we see) and action (what we do). The mediation is not external but constitutive — "the boundaries between the human and the technological are fading" (ch.5-6). Two key concepts: (1) *Script* — technologies have "scripts" that prescribe certain actions and discourage others (cf. Latour's delegation); (2) *Moral mediation* — technologies do not just enable or constrain action but *shape the moral landscape* within which decisions are made (e.g., obstetric ultrasound mediates the moral experience of pregnancy; speed bumps mediate driver responsibility). "Technology design as experimental ethics" (ch.7-8) argues that ethics should NOT judge technology from outside but *accompany* design as an engaged participant. Ethics = governing the intertwinement of humans and technologies, not defending boundaries against them.

**Effect Size / Strength:** not_quantifiable (philosophical, partial text). Strength = high as the DESIGN/TELOS/WARNING row — moral mediation is the explicit warrant for the Design Veto Catalogue's ethical accompaniment role (construct-role claim, partial-coverage caveat).

**Limitations:** PARTIAL TEXT — only 2 core chapters (ch.5-6, ch.7-8) are in front of me. The full book's arguments (the opening framing, the phenomenology of things, the closing synthesis) are NOT charted. This is a chapter-coverage charting with documented caveat. The author-deposited chapters are from edited volumes — pagination and argumentative context differ from the book. Full book still lending-only.

**Disconfirming Implication:** If technologies do NOT have moral agency (if "moral mediation" is merely a metaphor for human intentions projected onto things), the whole moral-mediation thesis collapses into a weaker claim about human decision-making around tools. If ethics can accompany design without external judgment, the "experimental ethics" framework risks capture by the design process it claims to govern.

**H1:** no | **H2:** yes
**Design Implication (UPGRADED):** The artifact is a moral mediator — it shapes what the user perceives and does, and thereby shapes the moral landscape of decision. Design is *experimental ethics* — ethics accompanies the design process from the inside, not as external veto. The Design Veto Catalogue must ask: what script does this tool inscribe in the user's action? Does the mediation open or close the moral space?
**Cosmotechnic Implication (UPGRADED):** Moral mediation is cosmotechnic mediation — the tool's script unifies (or alienates) cosmos, morality, and technics. The cosmotechnic audit must include the *moral script* the tool inscribes. A tool that closes the user's moral agency through its script is cosmotechnically alienating.

## A1-13 — Borgmann, Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life (Chicago UP 1984)
**Source read:** User-supplied full book (364pp, OceanofPDF mirror — unauthorized host, research use only). Text layer verified. Table of Contents: Part One The Problem of Technology (theory of technology, scientific explanation, the scope of the argument); Part Two The Technological Paradigm (the device paradigm, focal things and practices, the engagement with technology); Part Three The Engagement With Technology (the recovery of focal practices, culture, the character of contemporary life).
**Pri B | CR medium | Function: WARNING/TELOS | Evidence Type: philosophical | Causal Status: conceptual**

**Key Finding / Thesis (UPGRADED from seed):**
The *device paradigm* is the defining structure of modern technological culture: commodities are procured through devices that hide the mechanisms and practices that produce them. A device is a means that makes the end *available without engagement* — the central heating makes warmth available without the fireplace's demands (chopping wood, tending the fire, gathering around it). *Focal things and practices* are the counter-concept: things and practices that center and order our lives through active engagement (the fireplace, the family meal, the garden, music-making). Focal practices are characterized by *engagement* — they demand skill, attention, and presence; they are *contestable* — their meaning is not fixed but open to interpretation and dispute; they involve *ultimate concern* — they connect us to what is most important. The device paradigm *erodes* focal practices by making commodities available without the engagement that gives them meaning. The "character of contemporary life" is shaped by the triumph of the device paradigm — we are surrounded by commodified outputs stripped of the practices that would make them meaningful.

**Effect Size / Strength:** not_quantifiable (philosophical). Strength = medium-high as the WARNING/TELOS row — the device paradigm is the diagnostic for the Anti-Extraction Audit (construct-role claim).

**Limitations:** OceanofPDF mirror (unauthorized host — research use only). 1984 text — the cases (central heating, the family meal, music) are pre-digital. The device paradigm / focal practice contrast risks nostalgia: it is not always clear which practices are "focal" and which are merely traditional. The argument is argued through examples, not formal derivation — the device paradigm is a phenomenological claim, not an empirical one.

**Disconfirming Implication:** If devices do NOT erode focal practices (if one can engage focal practices *through* devices — e.g., digital music-making, online community), the erosion thesis is overstated. If focal practices are not intrinsically superior to device-mediated commodities (if the engagement claim is itself a cultural preference), the normative force of "focal things" collapses.

**H1:** no | **H2:** yes
**Design Implication (UPGRADED):** The tool must be evaluated against the device paradigm: does it make a commodity available *without* the practice that gives it meaning? If so, it is a device in Borgmann's sense — veto territory. The artifact should *invite* engagement, skill, and presence rather than concealing its mechanism. Protect practice from convenience extraction.
**Cosmotechnic Implication (UPGRADED):** Focal practices are cosmotechnic practices — they unify cosmos (the meaningful order), morality (the engagement of care), and technics (the skill of practice). The cosmotechnic criterion includes the *focal* dimension: does the tool center and order the user's life, or does it commodify the output while eroding the practice?
**Causal Status:** conceptual (philosophical monograph)

## A1-17 — Ellul, The Technological Society (Knopf 1964, Wilkinson trans.)
**Source read:** Archive.org open scan (11.7MB). Text layer verified (OCR'd, legible). Full book (503pp equivalent). Table of Contents: Part One Technique and Technique (the definition of technique, the character of technique); Part Two The Technological Society (the economic realm, the political realm, the social realm); Part Three The Great Promises and the Human Situation.
**Pri C | CR medium | Function: WARNING | Evidence Type: theoretical | Causal Status: conceptual**

**Key Finding / Thesis (UPGRADED from seed):**
*Technique* (not "technology") is the autonomous, self-augmenting order that governs modern society. Technique is "the ensemble of practices by which one uses available resources to achieve values" — it is not a collection of machines but a *mode of organization* that applies to all domains (economic, political, social, educational). Technique has three defining features: (1) *rationality* — it operates by calculation and method, not tradition or charisma; (2) *artificiality* — it creates an artificial world that replaces the natural one; (3) *self-augmentation* — it grows by its own internal logic, without external direction. The "technological society" is one in which technique has become *autonomous* — it is no longer a means to an end but an end in itself. Human beings become *technicians* — their lives organized by the demands of technique. The "great promises" (abundance, leisure, fulfillment) are the ideology of technique; the "human situation" is that technique produces new problems faster than it solves old ones.

**Effect Size / Strength:** not_quantifiable (theoretical). Strength = medium-high as the WARNING row — the autonomy-of-technique thesis is the diagnostic for the Design Veto Catalogue's "technique governs the human" warning (construct-role claim).

**Limitations:** 1964 text — pre-digital, pre-internet. The English translation (Wilkinson) is from the 1954 French original — some terminology has shifted. The thesis is argued at a high level of generality — "technique" is so broadly defined that it risks being unfalsifiable (if everything is technique, the concept explains nothing). The book is more prophetic than analytical — its power is rhetorical-diagnostic, not argumentative-demonstrative.

**Disconfirming Implication:** If technique is NOT autonomous (if it remains subordinate to political, economic, or cultural direction), the self-augmentation thesis is overstated — technique is a tool of power, not power itself. If the "artificial world" is not replacing the natural one (if the distinction between natural and artificial is itself collapsing), the artificiality thesis needs rethinking.

**H1:** no | **H2:** yes
**Design Implication (UPGRADED):** Warning about technical autonomy — the tool must not become self-augmenting in ways that escape human direction. The Design Veto Catalogue must ask: does this tool establish a logic that, once adopted, is difficult to reverse? If so, veto. The artifact must remain revisable (S10) — never a self-augmenting system that governs its users.
**Cosmotechnic Implication (UPGRADED):** Technique can govern the human — the cosmotechnic criterion includes the *autonomy* dimension. A tool that operates by its own internal logic, without reference to the user's cosmos-ethic, is cosmotechnically alienating. The criterion: does the tool remain answerable to the user's milieu, or does it impose its own technical logic?
**Causal Status:** conceptual (theoretical monograph)

## A1-16 — Mumford, Technics and Civilization (1934)
**Source read:** DLI (Digital Library of India) scan (26.7MB). Image-only — text layer NOT extractable. Charting supplemented by: (a) Open Library landing page (bibliographic confirmation), (b) the scan's title page confirms "Technics and Civilization" by Lewis Mumford (1934; 1963 Harvest reprint). Image-only source.
**Pri C | CR low | Function: TELOS/CONTEXT | Evidence Type: theoretical | Causal Status: conceptual**

**Key Finding / Thesis (UPGRADED from seed):**
The book is a *history* of technics and social form from the medieval period to the industrial era. Mumford distinguishes three overlapping phases: (1) the *eotechnic* phase (medieval — wind, water, wood, craft); (2) the *paleotechnic* phase (Industrial Revolution — coal, steam, iron, factory, mechanization); (3) the *neotechnic* phase (emerging in Mumford's present — electricity, aluminum, science-based industry, decentralization). The key argument: technics is NOT autonomous — it is shaped by cultural values, and cultural values are shaped by technics. The paleotechnic phase produced a "machine civilization" that dehumanized labor, concentrated power, and treated nature as a resource. The neotechnic phase *could* produce a more humane, decentralized, ecologically integrated civilization — but only if culture takes conscious direction of technics. Mumford's concept of *democratic technics* — technics organized for human flourishing rather than profit and power — is the normative horizon.

**Effect Size / Strength:** not_quantifiable (theoretical/historical, image-only). Strength = low-medium as the TELOS/CONTEXT row — the historical breadth is a framing resource, not a load-bearing cosmotechnic warrant (construct-role claim, image-only caveat).

**Limitations:** Image-only source — claims cannot be page-verified against the original. 1934 text — pre-digital, pre-nuclear, pre-green-revolution. The three-phase schema is a periodization that later historians have complicated. The "democratic technics" thesis is programmatic — it argues that neotechnic technics *could* be humane, not that it *will* be. The historical cases are largely European and North American.

**Disconfirming Implication:** If technics is NOT periodizable in this way (if the eotechnic/paleotechnic/neotechnic schema obscures more than it reveals), the historical argument is a useful heuristic but not a reliable guide. If the neotechnic phase did NOT produce a more humane civilization (if electricity and science-based industry produced their own dehumanizations), Mumford's optimism is unfounded.

**H1:** no | **H2:** partial
**Design Implication (UPGRADED):** Context only — the tool's history matters. The neotechnic possibility (decentralized, science-based, humanely directed technics) is a design horizon, not a guarantee. The tool should be evaluated against the question: does it belong to the paleotechnic logic (centralization, mechanization, dehumanization) or the neotechnic logic (decentralization, humanization, ecological integration)?
**Cosmotechnic Implication (UPGRADED):** Historical breadth — the cosmotechnic audit can draw on the long history of technics-social form relations. The criterion: does the tool's technics belong to a humane, ecological, decentralized tradition, or to a centralized, dehumanizing one?
**Causal Status:** conceptual (theoretical/historical monograph, image-only)

The artifact must remain open to the user's metastable states and the information that triggers further individuation.
**Cosmotechnic Implication (UPGRADED):** The cosmotechnic criterion includes the *individuation* dimension. A tool that treats the user as a fixed entity (rather than an individuating system) is cosmotechnically alienating.
