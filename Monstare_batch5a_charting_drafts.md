## A5-01 — Varela, Neurophenomenology (1996, Journal of Consciousness Studies)
**Source read:** DocsLib mirror (full article, 330-349). Text layer verified. Complete.
**Pri A | CR high | Function: METHOD/TELOS | Evidence Type: theoretical | Causal Status: conceptual**

**Key Finding / Thesis:**
First-hand experience is an irreducible field of phenomena — there is no "theoretical fix" or "extra ingredient" that can bridge the gap between cognitive science and conscious experience. Neurophenomenology seeks articulations by mutual constraints between phenomena present in experience and the correlative field of phenomena established by the cognitive sciences. The method requires a disciplined approach to first-person experience (micro-phenomenological interview, phenomenological reduction) that is rigorous enough to be reproducible. The "hard problem" can only be addressed by a research community armed with pragmatic tools enabling a science of consciousness. The structure of human experience itself is the only link between mind and consciousness that seems both obvious and natural.

**Effect Size / Strength:** not_quantifiable (theoretical/methodological). Strength = high as the METHOD/TELOS row — neurophenomenology is the explicit warrant for the Phenomenological Registration Sheet (construct-role claim).

**Limitations:** Single-author programmatic article; the method is argued for more than it is demonstrated. The "mutual constraints" concept is a research program, not a validated method. First-person methods remain contested in cognitive science. The article presupposes the irreducibility of experience (anti-reductionist commitment) rather than proving it.

**Disconfirming Implication:** If conscious experience IS reducible to neural or functional processes (if the "hard problem" is solvable "on the cheap"), the whole neurophenomenology program is unnecessary — cognitive science can proceed without first-person data.

**H1:** no | **H2:** yes
**Design Implication:** Justify phenomenological capture alongside metrics. The tool must capture first-person experience (pre-reflective, momentary) alongside behavioral/neural data — not as an afterthought, but as a structural requirement. The Phenomenological Registration Sheet is the artifact that operationalizes this.
**Cosmotechnic Implication:** Experience is data too. The cosmotechnic audit must include the subjective dimension — not just what the tool does, but what the user experiences. Phenomenology-before-calibration is law.
**Causal Status:** conceptual (theoretical/methodological article)

## A5-02 — Petitmengin, Describing Experience in the Second Person (2006, Phenomenology & Cognitive Science)
**Source read:** Author PDF (full article, 230-269). Text layer verified. Complete.
**Pri A | CR high | Function: METHOD | Evidence Type: methods | Causal Status: conceptual**

**Key Finding / Thesis:**
Describing one's own subjective experience is extremely difficult because a substantial proportion of experience unfolds below the threshold of consciousness. The micro-phenomenological interview (second-person method) enables a person — even untrained — to become aware of their subjective experience and describe it with great precision. The method uses specific inner gestures: conversion (shifting attention from the object to the act of knowing), evocation (re-living the episode), direct reference (pointing to the experience itself), attentional position, and speech position. The interview is focused on a specific episode (not general opinions), uses open prompts ("What were you doing?" not "Why?"), and validates the description through triangulation. The method has been used to study intuitive experience, epileptic seizure anticipation, and other cognitive processes.

**Effect Size / Strength:** not_quantifiable (methods). Strength = high as the METHOD row — the second-person interview is the explicit warrant for the Phenomenological Registration Sheet's interview protocol (construct-role claim).

**Limitations:** Methods article focused on the interview technique. The validity criteria for first-person descriptions remain contested. The method requires trained interviewers — it is not self-administering. The article presupposes that pre-reflective experience can be made reflectively accessible without distortion.

**Disconfirming Implication:** If pre-reflective experience is fundamentally inaccessible to reflective awareness (if the act of observation necessarily distorts the experience), the whole second-person method is unreliable — it produces post-hoc constructions, not faithful descriptions.

**H1:** no | **H2:** yes
**Design Implication:** Design episode-evoking prompts, not opinion prompts. The tool's registration interface must use open, specific, non-leading prompts that help the user re-live a particular episode rather than generalize about their experience.
**Cosmotechnic Implication:** Preserve fine-grained experience. The cosmotechnic audit must capture the texture of lived experience, not just summary judgments. The Phenomenological Registration Sheet must be designed for specificity.
**Causal Status:** conceptual (methods article)

## A5-05 — Hurlburt & Heavey, Descriptive Experience Sampling (2006, Phenomenology & Cognitive Science)
**Source read:** UNLV faculty PDF (full article, ~300pp equivalent). Text layer verified. Complete.
**Pri A | CR medium | Function: METHOD | Evidence Type: methods | Causal Status: conceptual**

**Key Finding / Thesis:**
Descriptive Experience Sampling (DES) is a method for exploring inner experience using random beepers in natural environments. When the beep sounds, subjects capture their inner experience at "the moment of the beep" — the last undisturbed moment before the beep interrupted. They jot notes, then report in an expositional interview within 24h. DES aims at "pristine" experience — experience in its natural state, unspoiled by the act of observation, unplanned, unmapped, uninterpreted. The method is fundamentally idiographic (characterizing one person's experience) but can be used nomothetically (across subjects with shared characteristics). Salient DES findings include: inner speech, visual images, unsymbolized thinking, sensory awareness, and feelings — these are the common forms of inner experience.

**Effect Size / Strength:** not_quantifiable (methods). Strength = high as the METHOD row — DES is the explicit warrant for the momentary sampling component of the Phenomenological Registration Sheet (construct-role claim).

**Limitations:** Methods article focused on the sampling technique. DES is labor-intensive (requires multiple sampling days, trained interviewers). The "pristine experience" aim is an ideal — all observation involves some interference. The method captures momentary experience but may miss extended, narrative, or temporal phenomena.

**Disconfirming Implication:** If inner experience is NOT best captured by random sampling (if the relevant phenomena are extended, narrative, or temporal rather than momentary), DES misses the phenomena that matter most for the Monstare protocol.

**H1:** partial | **H2:** partial
**Design Implication:** Semi-random in-the-moment state capture. The tool must include a random or semi-random beep that prompts the user to capture their experience at the moment of the beep — not retrospectively, not narratively, but in the pristine present.
**Cosmotechnic Implication:** Moment over narrative. The cosmotechnic audit must privilege the momentary, pre-reflective capture over retrospective narrative. The Phenomenological Registration Sheet must be designed for immediacy.
**Causal Status:** conceptual (methods article)

## A5-06 — Bass-Krueger et al., DES vs Micro-Phenomenological Interviews (2024, INDECS)
**Source read:** INDECS journal (full article, 84-106). Text layer verified. Complete.
**Pri A | CR medium | Function: METHOD/COMPARISON | Evidence Type: empirical | Causal Status: correlational**

**Key Finding / Thesis:**
Compares two first-person methods: Descriptive Experience Sampling (DES) and micro-phenomenological interviews (MPI). Both look at short episodes of experience and have safeguards to limit biases. But they differ in how they deal with memory, questioning, and analysis. DES results focused more on fine-grained details of visual experiences; MPI results focused more on how experience extended over time and how participants engaged with the task. These differences demonstrate how the applied methods differ in their focus and scope — neither is "wrong"; they reveal different aspects of experience. The study used a mental imagery task with 4 participants, each interviewed with both methods.

**Effect Size / Strength:** not_quantifiable (empirical, N=4). Strength = medium as the METHOD/COMPARISON row — the comparison is the warrant for choosing/integrating DES and MPI in the Phenomenological Registration Sheet (construct-role claim).

**Limitations:** Very small sample (N=4). The study is exploratory, not confirmatory. The mental imagery task is specific — generalizability to other domains is uncertain. The comparison is qualitative, not quantitative.

**Disconfirming Implication:** If the two methods do NOT differ in focus and scope (if they produce identical results), the integration argument collapses — you would only need one method.

**H1:** no | **H2:** partial
**Design Implication:** Choose immediate sampling vs episode interview. The tool must decide whether to use momentary sampling (DES), episode interview (MPI), or both — depending on the phenomenon of interest. For flow-state detection, DES-style momentary sampling is more appropriate; for retrospective sense-making, MPI-style episode interview is more appropriate.
**Cosmotechnic Implication:** Method fit to moment. The cosmotechnic audit must match the method to the phenomenon — momentary vs extended, pristine vs narrated.
**Causal Status:** correlational (empirical, N=4)

## A8-02 — Norton & Gino, Rituals Alleviate Grieving (2013, Journal of Experimental Psychology)
**Source read:** HBS-hosted PDF (full article). Text layer verified. Complete.
**Pri A | CR high | Function: EMPIRICAL/MECHANISM | Evidence Type: empirical | Causal Status: causal**

**Key Finding / Thesis:**
Rituals reduce grief via increased sense of control, even without belief. Participants who were directed to reflect on past rituals or who were assigned to complete novel rituals after experiencing losses reported lower levels of grief. Increased feelings of control after rituals mediated the link between use of rituals and reduced grief after losses, and the benefits of rituals accrued not only to individuals who professed a belief in rituals' effectiveness but also to those who did not. The specific rituals in which people engage after losses vary widely by culture and religion — and among participants — but the common psychological mechanism is regained feelings of control.

**Effect Size / Strength:** causal (lab-bounded, experimental). Strength = high as the EMPIRICAL/MECHANISM row — the control-restoration mechanism is the explicit warrant for the Ritual Transition Cards' regulatory function (construct-role claim).

**Limitations:** Lab-bounded experimental studies — the losses studied (death of loved ones, end of relationships, lottery losses) are recalled, not induced. The sample is US university students — generalizability to other cultures and populations is uncertain. The mechanism (control restoration) is proposed but the specific ritual features that produce it are not fully specified.

**Disconfirming Implication:** If rituals do NOT increase feelings of control (if the mechanism is something else — e.g., social support, cognitive reframing, mere distraction), the control-restoration thesis is wrong and the design implications need revision.

**H1:** partial | **H2:** partial
**Design Implication:** Ritual for loss/interruption/closure/return. The tool must include ritual structures that restore the user's sense of control during transitions, interruptions, and losses. The Ritual Transition Cards are the artifact. The ritual need not be religious or belief-dependent — the mechanism is control restoration, not faith.
**Cosmotechnic Implication:** Control through symbolic action. The cosmotechnic audit must include the ritual dimension — the symbolic-technical practices that restore the user's sense of control during disruption.
**Causal Status:** causal (lab-bounded, experimental)
